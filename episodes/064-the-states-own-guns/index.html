South Africa's police lose 49 guns a month

On the night of 26 September 2026 gunmen killed 17 people in a tavern outside Johannesburg and 10 more near Cape Town. Where those guns came from is not yet known.

Ten years earlier, police traced 888 gang guns, linked by forensic tests to more than 1,000 murders, back to a police store where they were waiting to be destroyed. Many had already been recorded as destroyed.

Destroyed on paper. Still killing.

Suspect one: the gun law is weak

On paper South Africa's law is one of the stricter ones: a licence, a competency test and an entry in the national register, and a draft bill (on hold) would remove self-defence as a reason to own a gun. One 2026 investigation put the domestic share of illegal guns at 99%: most started as legal guns, civilian or state. Every civilian owner is checked by someone else. Who checks the state?

Suspect two: a few corrupt officers

Colonel Chris Prinsloo admitted selling around 2,000 guns from police stores to gangs and was sentenced to 18 years in 2016. But between April 2022 and mid-2026 the police lost or had stolen 2,523 of their own guns, about 49 a month — and they are one of more than 500 state bodies holding guns, which are required to report losses and, a researcher found, "simply don't".

Forty-nine a month

SAPS guns lost or stolen, April 2022 – mid-2026: 2,523 (parliamentary reply, The Citizen, 19 Aug 2026). One square = one gun.

Every check checks itself

A civilian gun is licensed, registered and can be seized by someone other than the owner: the police. The police's own guns are checked at three moments — count, destruction, recovery — and at each the checker is the one being checked. The register is run by the police; destruction is certified by the holders (in the Cape Flats case, according to investigators, a second officer signed for guns never destroyed); a court-ordered list showed 35,202 guns "destroyed" without certificates; the Auditor-General, who reads reports but does not open stores, found recoveries counted twice.

The auditor problem

An auditor is a person who can be paid or threatened, and an outsider visits once a year while the store is open every day. In the Cape Flats case there already was a second signature; according to investigators, it was false. Which designs still work when the people inside them cannot be trusted?

Three designs, three prices

Leave nothing to steal. UN standards: destroy seized illegal guns rather than store them, witnessed and checked. Kenya burned 6,000+ guns in public in June 2025. Price: evidence and reach — 16,000+ SA guns were destroyed without the required ballistic test, there is no evidence burning reduces leaks, and it does nothing for guns in use.

Let every leak sign its name. Brazil (Law 10.826/2003) marks police ammunition by batch and buyer; the cartridges in Marielle Franco's murder led to a batch sold to the federal police. Price: precision and laboratory time — that batch was about 1.86 million rounds across 27 offices, and SA's ballistics laboratory had 41,000+ cases waiting in late 2025.

One batch, twenty-seven offices

Lot UZZ-18 sold to Brazil's Federal Police, 29 Dec 2006 (Metrópoles).

Never one person alone. The US two-person rule for nuclear weapons: no one near them alone, each able to spot the other's error. Applied to armouries: two people from different chains of command for every opening and every write-off. Price: staff at every store across 500+ bodies — and two can still agree.

Two keys, one hand

US DoD two-person concept; Cape Flats case per investigators.

Pick a design and read its bill

The question to carry

Nobody yet knows where the guns in that tavern came from, and under the current rules nobody outside the state could check.

Which price will a parliament pay for checks that rest on no one's word — evidence, precision and laboratories, or staff — and who will check the people who carry them out?

Not who's to blame. How it's built.
