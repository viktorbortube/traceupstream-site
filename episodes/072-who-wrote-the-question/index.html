400,000 signed a question. It never reached the ballot

In 2026 a campaign for Alberta's independence started collecting signatures to force a referendum on leaving Canada. Thomas Lukaszuk started a rival petition, meant to settle the matter the other way. It asked one plain question: "Do you agree that Alberta should remain in Canada?" Elections Alberta verified 404,293 signatures, far more than the law required.

The law did what it promised: the petition led to a vote, held on 19 October 2026. But the ballot did not carry the petition's question. It offered two options written by the government: A, Alberta remains a province of Canada; B, the government starts the legal process toward a second, binding referendum on separation. Lukaszuk said: "It is the premier's question. It's not our question."

The signatures bought a vote. Who decided what the vote would ask?

Signatures buy a vote, not the words

Lukaszuk's petition was meant to take leaving off the table. Earlier that year, on 13 May 2026, a court had quashed the approval of the independence campaign's petition. Yet the ballot put a path toward leaving right next to staying. Premier Danielle Smith said that a choice between A and B respected the wishes of both sides. Whatever one thinks of that, the people who decided what Albertans would be asked were not the people who had signed. On the same ballot, set by order-in-council, the government put nine more questions of its own.

So Alberta's law gives citizens the power to force a vote, but it leaves the pen, the power to write the question, with the government, which has its own stake in the answer. In unofficial results, about three in four Albertans chose to stay. The petition's side won anyway. So does it matter who holds the pen?

Words move votes

In 2013 Scotland's government planned to ask: "Do you agree that Scotland should be an independent country?" Britain's Electoral Commission, which by law reviews every referendum question, tested the wording on voters. People told it that "do you agree" nudged them toward yes, and the question became "Should Scotland be an independent country?" One poll found that simply adding "or disagree" moved the result by about two points. In 2015 the same commission found a second kind of tilt in the question on the European Union: yes was also the answer that kept things as they were. The ballot became a choice between Remain and Leave.

Three edits by a referee

Try it: read the question both ways

On Alberta's main question, a few points would not have changed a result of three to one. But the pen did something else there: it decided which questions exist at all. One of the nine questions asked whether the government should work with other provinces to abolish the unelected federal Senate, and a little over half said yes. In 2014 the Supreme Court of Canada ruled that abolishing the Senate takes the agreement of the federal Parliament and all ten provinces. A vote like that can still be used as a mandate in talks, but it binds no one who could actually do it.

If the pen carries this much weight, who should hold it?

The pen with the signers

Switzerland gives the pen to the signers. If 100,000 citizens sign within eighteen months, their own text goes to a national vote, and parliament cannot change a word of it. It may only recommend a yes or a no, or put up a counter-proposal.

Three places to put the pen, and the price of each

On 11 March 2012 a citizens' initiative proposed that in any town no more than one home in five could be a holiday home. It passed with 50.6 percent, and the sentence went into the constitution exactly as the signers wrote it. Then parliament wrote the law to carry it out. That law, in force from 2016, exempted every home that already existed on the day of the vote: those could still be turned into holiday homes.

Price: even when the signers hold the pen, the government picks it up again after the vote, in the law that carries the answer out. If the pen ends up with the government anyway, can someone at least check what it writes?

The pen with a referee

That is the British referee from the Scottish question. Its price is in what it does not decide. It checks whether the words are clear and fair, but the government still decides whether there is a vote at all, when it is held, and what it is about, and the commission's report is only advice. A referee checking for clarity would have let Alberta's Senate question through untouched, because that question was perfectly clear. It just could not be carried out.

A referee strong enough to block a question has to be chosen by someone. Canada already has one for a single kind of question. Under the Clarity Act, if a province asks its voters directly whether to leave, the House of Commons in Ottawa has thirty days to rule whether the question is clear. Alberta's ballot asked about starting a process, not about leaving itself. And the referee for that one question is the federal Parliament, the very side the province would be leaving.

So what if there were no question to word at all?

No question at all

Ireland comes close. Every change to its constitution has to be put to the people, and every ballot asks the same sentence: "Do you approve of the proposal to amend the Constitution contained in the undermentioned Bill?" Below it is the name of the bill, and the bill holds the new text itself. Vote yes, and that text becomes part of the constitution.

There is nothing in the question to tilt, and the answer binds. But the pen has not disappeared. Only the government and parliament can write that text, and in Ireland no number of signatures can put a question on the ballot. The 400,000 people who signed in Alberta would have had no pen at all.

The question to carry

Signers, a referee, or the law itself: each of them moves the pen, and none of them puts it down. So the next time you are handed a ballot, there are two questions on it that are never printed. Who wrote the question you are reading? And when you answer it, who is bound by what you said?

Then ask one more, about the place where you live: who holds the pen there?

Not who's to blame. How it's built.
