Why America can't stop changing the clocks

By 2026, nineteen American states had passed laws to stop changing their clocks. Florida went first, in 2018. Every one of those states still moved its clocks twice a year. Arizona stopped in 1968, and has stayed stopped.

In July 2026 the House of Representatives voted 308 to 117 to end the switch for the whole country; by October the Senate had not voted on it.

What does Arizona have that Florida doesn't?

Suspect one: someone is blocking it

In 2026 the House said yes and the Senate has not voted. But in March 2022 the Senate passed the same idea by unanimous consent, and the House never held a vote. Each chamber has said yes, four years apart, and each time the other one stopped. An obstacle that moves between buildings is rarely one person.

Suspect two: Americans can't agree

On the switch itself they agree: in an AP-NORC poll in October 2025 only 12% of adults supported changing clocks twice a year. The split comes one step later, when you have to keep one clock forever: summer time (daylight saving time, light late into the evening) or standard time (light early in the morning).

Suspect three: let the states decide

The Uniform Time Act of 1966 (15 U.S.C. §260a) sets the dates when clocks change and gives a state one way out: it may exempt itself and stay on standard time all year. That is how Arizona and Hawaii stopped. What a state may not do is stay on summer time all year.

All nineteen states that voted to stop wanted summer time. So their laws are promises that take effect the day Congress allows it; Delaware's waits on Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Maryland too.

A door that opens one way

15 U.S.C. §260a; H.R. 139 as passed by the House, 14 Jul 2026 (govinfo).

Suspect four: the reform will fix it

The bill the House passed (H.R. 139) puts the whole country on summer time for good and takes away the way back to standard time. Only places already exempt (Arizona, Hawaii and the territories) may keep their old clock. Under the old law and the new bill alike, Washington decides which direction a state may go.

The last suspect: where you stand in the zone

Boston and Detroit share a time zone. Detroit is about 12 degrees of longitude further west, so its sun rises 48 minutes later. On 3 January the sun rises in Boston at 7:14 and in Detroit at 8:02. On permanent summer time, Detroit's January sunrise would come at 9:02, Indianapolis at 9:06, Boise at 9:18.

China spans about five time zones by geography and has kept one clock, Beijing time, since 1949. In Kashgar the January sun rises at about 10:17. In Xinjiang many Uyghurs keep an unofficial local time two hours behind Beijing.

Zone lines are moved by the Department of Transportation when a county petitions, and the statutory test is the convenience of commerce (15 U.S.C. §261), not sunrise.

Three clocks, three prices

No clock adds a minute of daylight. Each only decides which end of the day gets it.

Summer time all year, chosen in Washington (H.R. 139). What nineteen state legislatures voted for, and what most people chose in the October 2025 poll. Price: winter mornings. America tried it in January 1974 as an oil-crisis emergency measure planned for two years; support fell from about four in five to two in five by February and Congress ended it before the year was out.

Standard time all year. Russia moved to it in 2014 after three years of permanent summer time, when Moscow's December sun rose close to ten; Mexico chose it in 2022. Price: a 4:07 June sunrise in Boston, summer evenings an hour shorter, and a choice against the majority of that poll. Mexico's border towns kept switching to stay in step with the US.

Each state chooses (H.R. 9638, introduced July 2026: standard time by default, states may move forward all year or seasonally; close to the EU Commission's 2018 proposal). Price: a patchwork; across a zone line, two hours between neighbours. The European Parliament voted to end the switch in March 2019 (410–192); by 2026 the member governments had not agreed. In Arizona the Navajo Nation switches and the Hopi reservation inside it does not.

The question to carry

Arizona had nothing Florida lacks but the direction: it wanted the clock the 1966 law lets a state keep. Most people want to stop changing the clocks. The disagreement is which clock to keep, and the answer lands differently depending on where your town sits in its time zone.

Under your clock, what time will the sun rise here in January, and set here in June? And if each state may choose for itself, what happens at the border with the next one?

Not who's to blame. How it's built.
