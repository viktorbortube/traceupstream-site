How 20 years of storage became a 7-day emergency

In November 2026, in Middletown, Iowa, a town of about 320 people, two streets were told to leave their homes for the day. The school would close, and firefighters would hose down the playgrounds afterwards. Just outside town, the US Army was going to blow up about 9,500 pounds of lead azide, the explosive that goes inside detonators. Warnings about lead dust went out to towns as far as a hundred miles away.

Seven days, twenty years

Emergency RCRA permit effective 13 November 2026; blast planned for 20 November (EPA; Pen City Current). Storage of about 20 years (Iowa Capital Dispatch; CNN).

From the permit to the planned blast was about a week, and the public had no days at all to comment. The explosive had been sitting there for twenty years.

So how did all that time run out before anyone could be asked?

The last week was by the book

The obvious answer is that somebody in the last week was careless: the Army moved too fast, or the regulator signed whatever it was handed. But look at what the last week actually was.

There are 68 drums, about 9,500 pounds of lead azide. It had been kept in two underground concrete tanks under a bath of ethanol and water, because wet lead azide is calm. In March 2026 an inspection found that the bath had dried up and the powder was turning into crystals. Crystals of lead azide can go off from friction or a knock. Moving the drums could set them off. So could leaving them.

What the inspection found

Schematic, not to scale. EPA On-Scene Coordinator; Iowa Capital Dispatch.

The Environmental Protection Agency agreed that this was an imminent danger and gave the Army an emergency permit to destroy the drums where they were. Even one of the environmental groups now suing over the permit said the Army may have no safer option left.

If the last week was done by the book, the problem is not in the last week. So where did the twenty years go?

A rule that wakes up late

American law on hazardous waste is strict. To store it, treat it or destroy it you need a permit, and getting one means a public process: at least 45 days for anyone to comment, and a choice of method that can be questioned.

But a stored military explosive is not waste. Under the Military Munitions Rule of 1997, it only counts as waste in two kinds of situation: the military itself decides to get rid of it, or it has decayed so badly that it can never be used again.

Unless the owner decides to act, the strict rules start only once the material is already ruined. Until then, how it is kept is left to the military's own internal rules, and nothing in the waste law asks anyone to set a date for destroying it, or to tell the neighbours what is inside.

When the waste rules start

40 CFR 266.202(b): when a munition becomes waste; 40 CFR 124.10: 45-day comment.

And that applies to a lot more than one basement. The Defense Department keeps a stockpile of old and unusable munitions waiting for destruction. In 2017 it was about 430,000 tons, with around 60,000 tons added every year, and no outside rule sets a date for any of it.

So what happens when the strict rules finally do arrive?

The emergency route

They arrive in emergency form. The emergency permit is meant for sudden dangers, like a spill or a fire, when waiting would put people at risk. It still has to be announced, but there is no comment period and no hearing, and the usual requirements apply only as far as they fit the emergency.

Two routes to a permit

    What started the clock elsewhere

    In Louisiana, an accident. After a blast at a private contractor's site in 2012, about 15 million pounds of M6, the powder that fires artillery shells, were left behind at Camp Minden. The plan agreed with the EPA was to burn it in the open. Residents organised, scientists joined them, and in 2015 the plan switched to a closed chamber that filtered the smoke. The price: months more with the danger still there, and millions for the chamber. And it only worked because the powder could still be moved. In Iowa, it could not.

    In Switzerland, a number in a law. Near the village of Mitholz, an old army depot still holds about 3,500 tons of wartime explosives inside a mountain. In 2017 the army was planning to put a computer centre there and ordered a fresh risk study. It found the danger well above the limit set in the Swiss law on major accidents, so the government had to act. It decided to dig everything out slowly and move the village out while it does. The price: about 170 people leave their homes for roughly ten years, and a credit of CHF 2.6 billion.

    For chemical weapons, a date in a treaty. The Chemical Weapons Convention gave the United States until 2007 to destroy its stockpile. Communities in Kentucky and Colorado fought the plan to burn the weapons near their homes, and in 1996 Congress froze the money for those incinerators and ordered other methods. The price: the public won its say and the date was missed. The last weapon was destroyed in July 2023, at a total cost of about $40 billion.
