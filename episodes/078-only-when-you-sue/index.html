The environmental law that only works when you sue

Off the coast of Massachusetts, a company planned America’s first offshore wind farm. It spent sixteen years in permits, hearings and lawsuits, and in 2017 it gave up. Nothing was ever built.

In North Dakota, an oil pipeline runs under a lake. In 2020, a federal judge cancelled its permit to be there. The oil kept flowing.

A wind farm and an oil pipeline, on opposite sides of almost every argument about energy. And yet both fights went through the same law, written in 1970. In November 2026, the US Senate voted to change exactly the part of it that did the stopping.

What kind of law can be turned against both, and what happens when you take that part away?

The usual suspects

The usual explanation is that environmental groups sue to block anything they dislike. But look at who fought the wind farm. The most determined opponents were people who owned homes along the shore, and some of the richest families in the country, who argued that the turbines would spoil the view from the coast. So the lawsuit does not belong to one side. Anyone with a stake in a project can use it, whatever the reason.

The second explanation is that government agencies keep losing in court. They don’t. When their environmental studies are challenged, the agencies win about eight cases out of ten. But even a case the agency wins can take around four years to settle.

The agency usually wins. The project waits anyway.

NEPA appeals 2013–2022: agencies won about 80%; about 4.2 years from the study to the end of the appeal (Breakthrough Institute, Understanding NEPA Litigation).

If the lawsuits mostly fail, why does the project have to wait at all?

A law that forbids nothing

Because of what kind of law this is. The National Environmental Policy Act, usually called NEPA, says that before a federal agency approves a road, a pipeline or a power line, it has to study what the project will do to the environment, and publish what it found.

That is all it requires. It does not say which choice to make. In 1989 the Supreme Court put it plainly: the law forbids decisions made without looking, not decisions that are unwise. An agency can approve a harmful project, as long as it has studied the harm.

So how does a law that forbids nothing ever stop anything? Through the courts. If a judge finds the study was incomplete, the standard answer has been to cancel the approval until the study is done properly. And while a case is still open, the approval could be cancelled at any moment, so builders and lenders usually wait to see how it ends, even when the agency goes on to win.

So the court case is the law’s only brake. And it is blind. It does not ask whether the project is a wind farm or a pipeline. It only asks whether the project can still be made to wait.

The pipeline under the lake was already built. When its permit was cancelled, an appeals court refused to order it emptied, and the oil kept flowing. The wind farm had nothing built yet, and the waiting killed it.

So what happens if you take the waiting away?

Taking out the teeth

That is what the Senate’s bill does. It passed 62 to 33, with votes from both parties, and was sent to the House.

It changes who can pull the brake, and when. First, a lawsuit has to be filed within 150 days of the approval, instead of the six years that usually apply to suing the government. Second, you can only sue over problems you raised during the public comment period, so only people who spoke up in time can pull the brake at all. And third, the biggest change: if a judge finds the study was wrong, the approval stays in force. Building continues, and the agency gets no more than 180 days to fix the study.

After a judge finds the study was wrong

S.5653 as passed by the Senate: remand without vacatur, cure period of up to 180 days; 150-day filing limit; claims limited to issues raised in comments (CRS R49506; C2ES).

Try it: a judge finds the study was wrong. What happens next?

    Supporters point out that under the old rule, even a lawsuit that was going to lose could hold a power line, a housing project or a wind farm for years. Opponents ask a simple question. If a bad study can no longer stop anything, why would anyone write a careful one? And, they add, a study corrected after the concrete is poured can describe the damage, but it can no longer prevent it.

    Is there a way to keep some power to stop a bad project, without that power being years of waiting?

    Where the brake sits elsewhere

    Other countries put the brake in different hands, and each one pays for it.

    Britain keeps the power to cancel, but shortens the window far more than the American bill does. A big infrastructure approval can be challenged in court for only six weeks. Within that time, a judge can still cancel it. The price is time for the people affected: a problem discovered in week seven has no court left to hear it.

    Germany, in the gas crisis of 2022, let parliament name the projects itself. A special law listed new terminals for importing liquefied gas, and allowed them to skip the usual environmental assessment. The first terminal was built in about ten months. Here there was no study that could turn out to be wrong, so the choice was made by politicians in the middle of a crisis. Environmental groups say the work damaged the seabed, and without an assessment, there is no baseline to check that against.

    And America already has a law of a different kind. The Endangered Species Act does not ask agencies to look. It draws a line: you may not wipe out a species. In 1978, the Supreme Court used it to stop a dam in Tennessee that was more than two-thirds built, to protect a small fish. That is what no judge could do to the finished pipeline. The price of a line that hard is that it can stop a project after most of the money is spent, and so politics goes around it. A year later, Congress exempted that one dam from every federal law, and the gates closed.

    Who holds the brake

    A law that only asks you to look needs something to make looking matter. For more than fifty years, that something was the wait.

    Whenever someone proposes to change how projects get approved, ask this: once a project is approved, what can still stop it if the study turns out to be wrong, and who gets to pull that brake?
