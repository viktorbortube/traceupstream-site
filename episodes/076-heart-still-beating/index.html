When are you dead? The law decides, not your body

In some American hospitals, a patient is declared dead because their heart has stopped for good. A few minutes later, surgeons start that same heart again, inside the same chest. Nobody in the room believes they have brought the patient back: they are keeping the heart, the liver and the kidneys alive for someone else.

Declared dead, then the heart beats again

Schematic. Controlled donation after circulatory death with thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion (TA-NRP).

The first national rules for this procedure were adopted in November 2026. One of them tells surgeons what to do with the arteries that carry blood to the brain: clamp them shut, and then cut them open above the clamp.

Was that patient dead?

Nobody is in a hurry

The first suspicion is that someone is cutting corners to get organs. But the decision that starts all this is not made by a transplant surgeon. A patient with no hope of recovery is on life support, and the family and the doctors caring for them decide to withdraw it, before anyone raises donation. Only after the heart stops does donation begin, and even then the team waits a few minutes to make sure the heart does not start again on its own. This is the no-touch period; when it ends, death is declared.

Restarting the heart inside the body lets surgeons see it working before they decide to use it, and the method has spread fast.

Everything depends on the moment death is declared. How long is the wait?

Two minutes or twenty

It depends on where you are. American protocols usually ask for two to five minutes. Spain, Britain and France: five. Austria and Switzerland: ten. In Italy the heart must show no electrical activity for twenty minutes. In Germany a stopped heart is not enough to become an organ donor at all; only the death of the whole brain is.

Try it: how long has the heart been still?

Minutes since it stopped

Medicine supplies the measurements: no pulse, no blood pressure, no electrical signal. Where the line falls on that clock is decided by a rule.

So what does the American rule actually say?

One word

Almost every American state has adopted the same model law, the Uniform Determination of Death Act of 1981. A person is dead when breathing and circulation have stopped, or when every function of the whole brain has stopped. In both cases the stop must be irreversible.

A heart that has been still for five minutes can often be started again. The lawyers who later studied the act put the difference like this: irreversible means it cannot be reversed; permanent means it could be, but it will not be. For decades doctors have treated "irreversible" as if it said "permanent". That reading worked as long as nobody restarted the heart. The new procedure does.

Two readings of one word

Blood comes back to the body, not to the brain

Schematic, not anatomy. OPTN rules adopted November 2026: clamp and cut open the arteries to the brain.

Defenders answer with the brain half of the law: if blood never reaches the brain, it cannot recover, so the patient is dead by the brain standard even with a beating heart. Some critics say the clamp does not always hold; one transplant surgeon has told reporters she has seen signs of life when blood did reach the brain. The federal official in charge of the transplant system answers that this is why the new rules add a second barrier.

If one word in the law is the trouble, why not change the word?

They tried

In 2021 the Uniform Law Commission, the body of state-appointed lawyers that drafted the original act, set up a committee to update it, and "irreversible versus permanent" was at the centre of the fight. In September 2023 it paused the work indefinitely, citing inertia, the political climate and the many consequences of changing how the law describes death.

So the word stayed as written in 1981. The national transplant network, which runs the system under a federal contract, adopted the new rules on 6 November 2026 as an emergency action, before the public was asked for comments. Legislatures voted on the word. Nobody voted on what it means.

Is there a way to draw this line that does not end up in the operating room?

Three places to draw the line

Where the line is drawn

Brierley et al., Frontiers in Transplantation 2024; country outlines: Natural Earth via world-atlas.

Australia reads the word literally. Its law speaks of the irreversible end of circulation in the body, so restarting the heart inside the body appears to be unlawful and is not used. In 2014 surgeons in Sydney were among the first in the world to restart a stopped heart outside the body, in a machine. The price: every such heart travels in a machine, and the liver and kidneys of the same donor get none of the benefit of blood restored inside the body.

Britain and the Netherlands draw the line around the brain. They allow the procedure in the abdomen, where it brings blood back to the liver and kidneys, but have paused the version that restarts the heart in the chest. The price is paid in hearts, which are run on a machine or not used, and the line follows anatomy rather than any definition of death.

Spain allows both, and decides who checks. One national agency writes the protocol and audits the cases; the arteries to the brain are clamped and opened before any blood is pumped, and the donor's brain is monitored throughout. The price is paid before death: with a separate consent from the family, a patient who is still alive can be given blood thinners and have tubes placed in their blood vessels for the sake of someone else. Many countries forbid exactly that.

Each of these countries chose a line. But who, in each of them, did the choosing?

The question to carry

The argument over this surgery usually comes as a yes or a no. But each of these countries answered a different question first: who gets to say where death begins.
