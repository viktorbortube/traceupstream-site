The flood warning stopped at the border.

On Thursday China warned Nepal about a lake at the border, and people moved uphill. Two days earlier a wall of water came down the same river and killed close to five hundred people. Nobody called. That is not an accident. It is a rule.

Seven minutes

At 08:37 on Wednesday a piece of glacier broke off a mountain in Tibet and fell more than a kilometre. Seismometers in Germany and the United States felt it within minutes. The ice and rock dammed a small river, the dam gave way, and the flood covered the first 22 km at roughly 190 km/h — about seven minutes to the border, ten more to the town of Syabrubesi.

Nepal's flood office learned the wave existed about sixteen minutes after it crossed — six minutes after Syabrubesi — and not from China, but from its own district office watching the river go past. Within minutes it sent more than half a million text messages downriver; the four gauges nearest the border were already gone. Downstream, the system worked. Upstream of the border it did not exist.

Two clocks

Physics gave seventeen minutes to Syabrubesi. The institutions used none of them.

The same bridge

Thirteen months earlier, in July 2025, a lake on top of a glacier in Tibet burst. That flood came down the same river, took out the same border bridge and killed eighteen people. Nepal's flood office said it had received no warning because there was no formal channel for one. By late August the two governments had agreed to share information about glacial lake risk.

Two weeks before this flood, a landslide blocked a neighbouring branch of the river on the Chinese side; Nepal found out when the water level dropped. Then, on Wednesday, the wave. The agreement existed and the warning still did not. So the problem is not whether the neighbours talk. It is what kind of thing the agreement is.

Who owns a number

A river gauge is a post in the water with a sensor on it. Every few minutes it produces a number, and the question that decides everything is: whose number is that? Everywhere in the world the answer is the same — the number belongs to the country the post stands in. No general law obliges a country to tell the country downstream what its rivers are doing. That duty exists only where two states have written it down and signed it.

A 1992 United Nations convention on shared rivers does exactly this: its Article 14 says riparian states must warn each other of a critical situation without delay and build joint alarm systems. China has not signed it. Nepal has not. Neither has India. What the two governments agreed last August was to share information — not who calls whom, within how many minutes, on which line. A courtesy is real, and this week it worked for a lake that sat there for a day. It cannot work for a wave, because a courtesy has no deadline in it.

Where the gauges stand

About 60% of the catchment lies across the line, with 28 lakes rated likely to burst. Every gauge Nepal can read is below it.

Way one — write the deadline in

In 1986 a chemical warehouse burned in Basel, and the water used to put it out carried tonnes of pesticide down the Rhine, killing fish for hundreds of kilometres into three other countries. The Rhine states wrote a rule: any country that sees an accident or an imminent flood must inform the others immediately. Main alarm centres, a model that predicts where the wave will be, drills to test that the phone gets answered — Article 14 running for forty years.

Price: the Rhine states handed each other their river data because none of them treats its stretch as a military secret. Tibet is a border zone, and a live feed of its rivers is a live feed of how its dams are run. A duty with a deadline hands the neighbour a lever over you. China has never signed one with India; India has not signed one with Bangladesh, nor Turkey with Iraq.

Way two — buy the number

Since 2008 India has paid China every year for the readings from three gauges on the Brahmaputra, with a clause about immediate notice of anything abnormal. In 2017 the two armies faced off in the Himalayas, and that flood season the data stopped — China said its stations were being upgraded; Bangladesh kept receiving readings from the same stations. The feed came back a year later, and it only ever runs from mid-May to mid-October.

Price: a purchased number is a number you can be cut off from, on a schedule the seller sets. Nepal would be a much smaller customer than India.

The Brahmaputra feed, 2008–2026

Five months a year of readings, and one year of none.

Way three — see it yourself

Seismometers a continent away felt the glacier fall within minutes — no treaty, no payment. Satellites worked out the cause of last year's flood from orbit. On the Mekong in 2020 a satellite study showed China's dams holding water back during a downstream drought; Beijing called it groundless and, six months later, agreed to share river data all year round after eighteen years of wet-season-only sharing. There was no treaty behind that. There was a picture anyone could see. Nepal could wire a seismic trigger on its own side straight to its own sirens.

Price: on Wednesday the first reading of that seismic signal was wrong — the experts called it an earthquake, and it took hours to reclassify. A trigger fast enough to matter must fire before anyone has checked, so it will fire for earthquakes, quarry blasts and landslides that dam nothing. After the third empty siren, the official who switches it off breaks no rule. Nepal's automated sirens at a glacial lake near Everest, installed ten years ago, are rusting; some have no batteries.

Three designs, three prices

Each answers a different question. None is free.

Which rule was written down

Nobody broke a rule on Wednesday. The public argument is whether the neighbour should have called. The three designs ask something else: whether a country upstream will bind itself with a deadline, whether it will sell you the number, or whether you will build a machine that sounds the alarm without asking anyone — and live with every time it sounds for nothing.

Twenty-eight more lakes above this valley and the next are rated likely to burst. Sixty percent of the land that drains into this river is across the line. So when the next wave comes, the useful question will not be why nobody called. It will be whether any of these three rules was written down in the meantime — and who agreed to pay its price.

Not who's to blame. How it's built.
