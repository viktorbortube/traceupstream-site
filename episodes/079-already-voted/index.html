The Congress voters just fired still makes laws

In March 2025, the German parliament changed the country’s constitution. It loosened the rule that limits government borrowing, and opened the way to hundreds of billions of euros for defence and roads. It needed two thirds of all members. It got 513 votes, 24 more than it needed.

But that parliament had already been replaced. Voters had elected a new one a little over three weeks earlier, and it was due to meet one week later. In the new one, the parties against the change would have had enough seats to block it. The vote was held in the old one, a week before the new one met, and it was legal.

In November 2026, American voters handed both chambers of Congress to the other party. The outgoing majority went back to work with almost two months left before the new Congress meets. Between an election and the day the winners take over, there is a window of a few weeks.

How much power is left inside it to a parliament the voters have just replaced?

The usual suspects

The first explanation is that this is a trick one side plays. But look at who has done it. In 1980, after Ronald Reagan won, the outgoing Democratic Congress passed the Superfund law for toxic waste sites, and a law protecting huge areas of Alaska. In 2010, after Democrats lost the House, the outgoing Congress repealed the ban on gay soldiers serving openly, and the Senate ratified a nuclear arms treaty with Russia. In Germany, the votes came from the big parties of the centre-right and centre-left, together with the Greens. So the window does not belong to one party. It belongs to whoever still holds the old seats.

The second explanation is that the window is harmless, a few weeks of paperwork between one government and the next. It used to be much longer. Before 1933, the old Congress and the old president stayed in office until March, four months after the vote. An amendment to the Constitution that year moved the handover to January.

From the vote to the handover

Twentieth Amendment, ratified 23 Jan 1933 (National Constitution Center). Germany: election 23 Feb 2025, new Bundestag constituted 25 Mar 2025. UK: election 4 Jul 2024, new prime minister 5 Jul 2024.

In the winter after the election of 1932, a banking panic arrived in the middle of those four months. Herbert Hoover, who had lost, still held the power. Franklin Roosevelt, who had won, did not have it yet. Hoover asked Roosevelt to commit to his policies in advance, and Roosevelt declined. By the morning Roosevelt was sworn in, banks in most states were closed or limiting withdrawals.

The long window was dangerous because too little got done. Why is a short one dangerous because too much can be done?

Two different days

Because two things end on different days: the mandate, which is the voters’ permission to govern, and the power to actually do it. The mandate changes hands on election day. The power to pass laws and confirm officials stays with the old parliament and the old government until the day of the handover. In between, the outgoing majority can do everything it could do the day before the vote.

And some of its members do it without the one thing that normally keeps a representative careful: the next election. Those who lost their seats, or who are retiring, will not face these voters again. And the majority as a whole has already been judged. Nothing it does in the window can change that verdict.

So the window can be used for more than finishing old business. In North Carolina in 2016, and in Wisconsin in 2018, the legislature, together with the governor who was leaving office, passed laws that cut the powers of the governor who had just been elected. In North Carolina, courts later struck some of those laws down. In Wisconsin, the state’s supreme court left most of them in place.

When the old side sets the new side’s powers

If the window can be used like that, why does any country leave it open?

Two months in Washington

Because the window is also where necessary work gets done. Look at what fills the American one in 2026. Government funding runs out on December 11, so the outgoing Congress has to pass something or the government shuts down. The Senate’s Republican majority has also added extra days to its calendar, to confirm about two dozen of the president’s judges and nearly a hundred other officials before it hands over in January.

Federal judges serve for life. A judge confirmed in the last weeks of one Senate may still be deciding cases thirty years later. Democrats did the same in December 2024, confirming Joe Biden’s judges in the weeks after they lost the Senate.

Supporters of using the window make a simple point. The Constitution gives every Congress a two-year term, not a term that ends when the votes are counted. The government still needs money in December, and a crisis does not wait for January. Critics answer that the voters have just said what they want, and the window lets a majority they have already judged decide anyway.

Both are describing the same rule. So what do countries put in its place?

Closing the window

Countries have tried three ways, and each one pays for it.

Britain closes the window with the calendar. Parliament is dissolved before the vote, so there are no members left to pass laws during the campaign. And the handover comes the next day: in July 2024 the election was on a Thursday, and the new prime minister was in Downing Street on Friday. What stops the old majority is simple: it no longer exists. The price is on the other side. A new government gets no weeks to prepare, and while Parliament is dissolved, there is no legislature to call on if something urgent happens.

Australia and New Zealand tie the outgoing government’s hands with a convention. Until the new government is sworn in, ministers avoid major policy decisions, big appointments and big contracts, and if something cannot wait, they consult the opposition first. What stops them is a promise; no court enforces it there. Belgium has a similar rule, and it shows the other price. After its election in 2010, a caretaker government ran the country for 541 days. For months it ran on temporary monthly spending, with no budget for the year, until the king asked it to draft one. A government that only handles current business can leave a country’s big decisions waiting for as long as the talks last.

The United States and Germany leave the window fully open. The outgoing members keep every power until the last day, and only the ordinary rules apply to them. The case for it is that a crisis does not wait for the handover. The parties behind the German vote argued exactly that: the country needed the money for defence now, not after months of talks to form a new government. The price is that the decision is made by a parliament the voters have already replaced, and the next chance to answer for it comes years after it is in force.

    Who holds the window

    An election ends a mandate in a single day. In Berlin the power took a month to follow, in Washington two months, and every country has quietly decided what happens in between.

    Also worth knowing: before the Twentieth Amendment, a newly elected Congress did not meet in regular session until thirteen months after the election, while the old one sat in a “short session” until 4 March. In America the weeks after an election are called the lame-duck session. Germany’s Constitutional Court rejected urgent applications to stop the March 2025 vote. Bundesrat approval followed on 21 March 2025.

    So the next time you vote, ask one more thing: between the day you vote and the day the people you chose take over, who can still make decisions that will outlive their term, and what stops them?
