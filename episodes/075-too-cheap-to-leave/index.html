Why Americans can't afford to move

Picture a family that bought a house in 2021 with a $400,000 mortgage at 3%. They pay about $1,690 a month. Five years later they want to move into an identical house one street over, for the same price. They would borrow exactly what they still owe, over the same 25 years they have left, on the same income. Their new payment would be about $2,590.

Same debt, same income, same kind of house

Illustrative calculation: $400,000 at 3.00% over 30 years (2021) = $1,686/month; balance after 5 years $355,625; same balance over 300 months at 7.33% (Freddie Mac average, October 2026) = $2,589. Principal and interest only.

That is $900 more every month for a move of one street. In October 2026 Americans bought homes at the slowest pace since 2010. New mortgages cost more than 7%, and almost half of the mortgages Americans already had were below 4%.

So what exactly would that family be paying $900 a month for?

The sellers vanished too

The obvious answer is that money got expensive: when rates rise, fewer people can afford to buy. But in America the sellers disappeared as well. Economists at the Federal Housing Finance Agency found that for every percentage point the market rate sits above the rate a homeowner already pays, the chance that they sell falls by nearly a fifth.

Rates on the mortgages Americans already had, against the rate on a new one

Shares of outstanding mortgages by rate, Q2 2026 (FHFA National Mortgage Database). New-loan rate: 7.33%, 30-year fixed average, October 2026 (Freddie Mac).

Between mid-2022 and mid-2024, they estimated, that effect alone stopped about 1.7 million home sales. With so few homes for sale, prices went up by more than the high rates had pushed them down.

Yet the family's debt doesn't change when they move. So why should their rate?

One way to lose it

An American mortgage is a stranger deal than "a loan is a loan". If rates fall, the family can pay off the old loan early, with no penalty, and take a cheaper one. If rates rise, they keep the cheap rate for as long as thirty years. There is only one ordinary way to lose it: sell the house. Almost every standard mortgage lets the lender demand the whole balance on the day the home is sold.

That was not always settled. In 1978 California's Supreme Court ruled that a lender could not use that clause unless the sale actually put its money at risk, and buyers there could take over a seller's cheap loan. In 1982 Congress overrode every state on this point: lenders may enforce the clause, whatever a state's laws or courts say. Most American mortgages are written on standard forms from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, two companies the federal government has run since 2008. Those forms include the clause.

The same law lists the moments when a lender may not call in the loan: when the house passes to a spouse or to children, when the borrower dies, after a divorce. So the law already decides who keeps a cheap rate when a house changes hands, and a family that moves is not one of them.

The obvious fix is to add them to the list. Who would pay for that?

The obvious fix

Since late 2025 the agency that oversees Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac has said it is studying ways to let homeowners take their mortgage with them. In 2026 bills to do it were introduced in Congress; by November 2026 none had passed.

The catch: most American mortgages are bundled into bonds and sold to investors, who paid a price that assumed each loan would end when its house was sold. Let loans travel with their owners and investors hold 3% for years longer than planned. On the next mortgage bonds they ask for a higher return, and the next family to borrow pays a higher rate.

So the cheap loan is worth something to somebody. How much, and whose is it?

What the loan is worth

After five years the family still owes about $355,000. Imagine someone wanted to buy their loan: the right to collect $1,690 a month for the next 25 years. With new loans paying more than 7%, an investor would pay only about $230,000 for it, about 65 cents for every dollar the family owes.

What the family owes, and what their loan is worth

Illustrative calculation: present value of $1,686/month for 300 months at 7.33% = $231,660 (65.1% of the $355,625 balance). Difference $123,965 = present value of the $902/month gap.

The difference, about $124,000, is what the $900 a month from the start is worth in today's money. It is a real asset, and the family owns it only while they stay. On the day they sell they repay the full $355,000, and that $124,000 goes to whoever holds the loan.

A rule decides whose money it is. Are there rules that give it to someone else?

It stays with the house

America already has one, on a small scale. Loans insured by the Federal Housing Administration or guaranteed by Veterans Affairs can be taken over by the buyer at the old rate.

Say the house sells for $450,000. The buyer takes over the $355,000 at 3%, but has to find the other $95,000 up front, or borrow it at the new rate. Because the loan is worth so much to whoever takes it, the family can ask more for the house. But only if a buyer comes along who wants that loan and has that much cash. And a loan that outlives its sale costs investors just as much as one that travels, so they price that in for everyone who borrows.

What if the family could collect that value from any buyer, or with no buyer at all?

It becomes money

Denmark does that. Every Danish mortgage is matched to a bond with exactly the same payments, and the family may pay off the loan by buying the matching bond on the market, at whatever it costs that day.

Pay off the loan at the bond's price

Illustrative calculation, the same family under Danish rules: buyback at 65.1 → new loan $231,660 at 7.33% over 300 months → $1,686/month. Denmark 2022: DKK 243bn (≈ $34bn) bought back at an average 18% discount (Danmarks Nationalbank).

In 2022 Danish fixed mortgage rates rose by about four percentage points, and homeowners bought back loans worth about $34 billion, on average 18% below what they owed.

The price is paid at the start. Danish loans live inside bonds that investors buy as close to risk-free, so Danish mortgage banks lend no more than 80% of a home's value. On a $450,000 house that leaves $90,000 to find from savings or from a costlier bank loan, where an American family can often put down $15,000–20,000. And it only works because each Danish loan is tied to a bond the family can find and buy; American mortgages are pooled by the thousand, and giving fifty million of them that link would mean rebuilding how the country pays for its homes.

Is there a way to stop the value from piling up in the first place?

Nothing to lose

That is what Canada does. A Canadian mortgage can usually be carried to the next home, but the fixed rate typically lasts only five years, and then the loan is renewed at whatever rates are by then. A promise that short never grows into a hundred-thousand-dollar asset.

One promise for thirty years, or six promises of five

Schematic. The Canadian steps illustrate renewal at market rates; they are not a forecast.

The price is that the family carries the rate risk itself. In 2025 the Bank of Canada estimated that about six in ten mortgages renewing in 2025 and 2026 would face higher payments; for five-year fixed loans, about 15–20% higher on average.

So which of these rules is the right one?

The question to carry

No rule makes the value disappear. It can go to whoever holds the loan, in exchange for a rate that never resets; to the family, through a buyer or through a bond; or to nobody, if the promise is kept short. And whoever doesn't get it pays for it.
