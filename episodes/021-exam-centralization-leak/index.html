Why one exam for 2.3 million
students was built to leak.

This week, India's education minister resigned — because of a single sheet of paper: the question paper for one exam. That exam decides who becomes a doctor in India. This year 2.27 million people sat it, for about 130,000 medical school places — one place for every eighteen students.

A sheet of paper with that much power is worth extraordinary money: the last time it leaked, copies reportedly sold for over ₹3 million each (~$36,000). The strange part: India created this exam deliberately, ten years ago, as a weapon against corruption. It worked — against the corruption it was aimed at. It also gathered two million families' hopes onto one document, and quietly turned the document itself into one of the most valuable pieces of paper in the country.

01
The Event
The event · India, May–July 2026

From exam day to a resignation, in twelve weeks.

Fig. 1 — twelve weeks, click each point for detail · sources: CNN, Al Jazeera, NPR, India TV

On 3 May, more than two million students took the national medical entrance exam — NEET. Nine days later the government cancelled it: a so-called guess paper — a coaching-shop prediction of likely questions, sold before exam day — matched the real questions too closely to be a guess. The case went to federal investigators, and every one of those students was told to take the exam again. The retake came in late June; within days new leak claims were circulating. The testing agency says those particular claims were fake. True or not, by then trust had run out.

Through July a protest grew into the largest youth movement India has seen in over a decade — the Cockroach movement, named after widely reported remarks in which a senior judge described unemployed young Indians as cockroaches and parasites. Engineer and educator Sonam Wangchuk went on a 26-day hunger strike; when police moved him into a hospital against his will, the crowds multiplied. On 25 July the education minister, Dharmendra Pradhan, handed in his resignation. The exam system itself did not change that day.

02
The Pattern
The pattern · 2024, then 2026

Ten years in prison did not stop it.

Fig. 2 — the 2024 chain, the law between, the 2026 repeat · sources: Careers360, Outlook, newsonair

Two years ago the same exam leaked the day before test day. Police traced it: an organised network had obtained the paper through a gap in how it was transported and stored, and sold it, copy by copy, at the price you heard a minute ago. That year's results showed 67 students sharing the very top rank — a result the exam had never produced before.

India's response sounded strong: a new law against exam fraud — up to ten years in prison, fines up to ₹10 million. That law was fully in force when the 2026 paper leaked anyway. And that is the detail worth pausing on: when you raise the punishment and the crime happens again on schedule, punishment is not the variable that matters. What matters is the price. A stolen paper is worth whatever buyers will pay, and the buyers are two million families staring at odds of one in eighteen. Against a prize like that, ten years of prison is not a wall. It is a cost of doing business.

03
The Reform
The reform · 2016, and what it replaced

Eighty exams became one — on purpose.

Fig. 3 — ~80 separate exams swept into one national gate · sources: Supreme Court Observer, sarvgyan

Before 2016, India did not have one medical entrance exam — it had around eighty, run by states and individual universities. That system was rotten in its own way: private colleges sold seats for so-called capitation fees — officially donations, sometimes tens of thousands of dollars. Families with money bought their children into medicine; families without watched it happen.

So in 2016 India's Supreme Court ordered a clean sweep: one national exam for every medical college in the country. One paper, one merit list, no side doors. The explicit goal was to end the corruption and the seat-selling — and on those terms it largely succeeded. But look at what the same decision did to the geometry of the system: eighty exams meant eighty smaller prizes, each worth stealing only to a local market. One exam means a single prize, national in scale. The money that once flowed through a college's back door did not disappear — the demand behind it moved upstream, to the one document that now controls everything.

04
The Mechanism
The mechanism · Stakes × crowd

The value of stealing an exam, in one line.

₹3.0Mvalue of one stolen paper 2,270,000futures hit by one leak 1sittings
The stakes per student don't change — only how many stand behind each paper. That alone divides the prize.
Fig. 4 — move the slider: the prize divides as the crowd splits · schematic, illustrating structure

The mechanism in one sentence: the value of stealing an exam equals the stakes for one student, multiplied by the number of students standing behind the same paper. You can make that sheet cheap in two ways — lower the stakes, or split the crowd. India's design does the opposite of both: the one score is the difference between a doctor's life and starting over, and every candidate in the country answers the same questions on the same morning.

That does two dangerous things at once. First, it sets up a contest the defenders have to win every single time: the guardians spend on sealed boxes, tracking, secure printing — the thieves only need one weak link, once, anywhere in a chain reaching thousands of exam centres. In 2024 the weak link was one storage room in one town. Defence has to be perfect everywhere; attack only has to succeed somewhere. Second — this is what turns a crime into a national crisis — the same centralisation that created the prize removed the walls between failures. When one of eighty exams leaked, one region re-sat a test. When the one exam leaks, 2.27 million people lose their exam on the same day, and every family in the country learns at the same moment that the system can be bought — exactly the belief that ended up on the streets of Delhi this month.

05
The Alternatives
What if · Time, chance, or space

Three ways to make the paper worth less.

Fig. 5 — three redlined redesigns · dashed green = revision markup · sources: JGME/PMC, iamexpat, College Board

Split the prize in time. In the US the college admission test runs seven times a year, and the version for graduate schools draws each student's questions from a rotating pool. Steal one paper and you have stolen one sitting of many; a suspicious score can be checked against other attempts. The cost: the whole security apparatus runs all year round for two million people at a time; sittings must be exactly equally difficult, and every difference becomes a lawsuit; and the American tests have had their own leaks in Asia — smaller prizes are still prizes.

A lottery above the bar. In the Netherlands, from the 1970s, you first cleared a qualifying bar — and above it, medical places were assigned by weighted lottery (top grades tripled your odds). If crossing the bar is what matters and the rest is chance, a leaked answer key buys far less. The cost is written in Dutch history: in 1996 a straight-A student was rejected three times, the country's sense of fairness revolted, the lottery was banned by 2017 — and in 2023 the ban was lifted, because the interviews that replaced it carried biases of their own. A society must say out loud: beyond a point, we cannot measure who deserves it more.

Split the prize in space. Many exams instead of one — the system India abolished. A leak in one place stays in one place. The cost is the reason NEET exists: scattered admissions meant seat-selling, capitation fees, a thousand small corruptions no investigator could see at once. India traded a thousand invisible failures for one enormous visible one. That was a trade — not a rescue.

The close · Stakes × futures

What is the one place worth to a thief?

The crowds in Delhi won what they asked for. But notice what did not change on the day the minister left: the exam is still one exam; the paper is still worth more than a house; the conditions for the next leak are all still in place. The comfortable version of this story has a villain. The uncomfortable version is about geometry: any system that pushes two million lives through one door, on one morning, makes the key to that door worth stealing at industrial scale. Guard it harder, and you have only made the theft more expensive — not impossible.

Every country decides how much of a life one test is allowed to determine, and how many futures stand behind each paper. Those two numbers, multiplied, are the bounty on that sheet of paper. So the next time a reform promises to end corruption by putting everything in one place — one exam, one register, one gate — it may well be right. Just ask the question India is asking this week: what will that one place be worth to a thief — and who pays on the morning it is stolen?
